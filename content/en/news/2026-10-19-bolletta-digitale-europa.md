Two studies by the economic analysis firm **Asterès**, commissioned by **Cigref** — the association of large French companies and public institutions — attempt to put a figure on something that usually stays an impression: **what it costs Europe to depend on American cloud and software.**

The numbers are large, and worth looking at closely. Not least because, as we shall see, they are not all of the same kind.

## First study: how much leaves

[*La dépendance technologique aux softwares & cloud services américains*](https://www.cigref.fr/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Etude-Asteres-La-dependance-technologique-aux-services-de-cloud-et-logiciels-americains-avril-2025.pdf) (April 2025, 33 pages) estimates that:

- European Union companies buy **€264 billion** a year of cloud and software services that benefit the American economy;
- American suppliers hold **around 83%** of the European cloud-software market — **€330 billion** for the Union, €54 billion for France alone;
- those €264 billion amount to **roughly 1.5% of EU GDP**, and to **one and a half times the European Union's budget**.

The most striking comparison is in the study itself: EU energy imports in 2024 came to **€360 billion**. Europe's digital bill, Asterès writes, is **"of an amount comparable to its energy bill"**.

On the American side, those purchases generate **$323 billion of value added** — 1.1% of US GDP — and **1,943,000 jobs**, or 1.2% of total US employment.

And the reverse? The study tries to quantify it: if the Union managed to produce **15%** of what it currently buys from the United States by 2035, the result would be **463,000 additional jobs** in Europe.

## Second study: how fast it grows

A year later, [*De la dépendance technologique à la captation économique*](https://www.cigref.fr/wp/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Cigref-Asteres-Hausses-Tarifaires-Cloud-Logiciel-Europe-2026.pdf) (May 2026, 34 pages) shifts from volume to **price**, and the picture worsens:

- cloud-software costs rose **+8.7% a year over the past three years**, and organisations expect **+12% a year over the next five**;
- **the largest increase faced at contract renewal averages 51%** — with extreme cases reported at **100%, and up to 200–300%**;
- the average excess cost for 2026–2030 is **€140 billion a year** at constant volumes;
- of which **€93 billion would leave the continent permanently** instead of funding the local economy. The study notes this is **the same order of magnitude as the annual digital budget required by the Draghi plan**;
- the overall estimated effect is a loss of **€107 billion of value added per year, 0.6 points of GDP, and 1.4 million jobs** by 2030.

One detail deserves to be pulled out, because it bears directly on how artificial intelligence is being sold: the increases are justified by **new AI features, imposed in 40% of cases**, while **around 60% of respondents judge the related productivity gains to be theoretical** in the short and medium term.

## What these numbers are not

Here a clarification is needed that almost nobody makes — and that the study itself makes honestly.

<div class="callout callout-highlight warning">
  <div class="callout-title"><span>How to read the figure</span></div>
  <p><strong>The €264 billion is not a measurement: it is an estimate extrapolated from interviews with six <em>Chief Information Officers</em></strong> of large companies, who reported cloud-software spending of around 2.2% of revenue, with 83% going to American suppliers. The French results were then extended to the Union in proportion to GDP.</p>
  <p class="mb-0">Asterès explains why it had to proceed this way: trade data is unusable because of <strong>Ireland's overwhelming weight in the flows</strong> — of $225 billion of European surplus in IT services in 2022, $194 billion was Irish — and foreign direct investment profit data is too imprecise.</p>
</div>

Something else should be said: **Cigref is an interested party.** It is the association of large French companies and public institutions, and it has a legitimate but evident interest in pushing this subject onto the European agenda. That does not disqualify the study — the methodology is stated and the limits are written down — but anyone citing it should know.

Finally, a distinction that matters particularly to us: the 2025 study measures **companies**, not public administrations. The 2026 one broadens to "organisations". The perimeter is therefore **adjacent to ours, not identical**.

## Why we keep building our own measurement

One could object: if Asterès has already done the sums, what is our second instrument for?

Because the two measurements answer the same question **from opposite directions**, and need each other.

Asterès measures **from above**: a sample of interviews, a macroeconomic extrapolation, a continental figure. It is the only way to get a European order of magnitude, and it carries the limit of any extrapolation — six interlocutors are not a census.

What we are building measures **from below**: public contracts actually awarded, body by body, year by year, from open data, with the method published and the figures reproducible by anyone. It will never produce a European figure. It will produce an Italian one that is **verifiable**.

> Where they have the order of magnitude and a sample of six, we would have the precise figure for Italian public administration. We are not competing with these studies: **we are their consistency check.**

And there is a political reason why the second measurement matters as much as the first. A continental number is impressive and unassailable precisely because it is abstract: nobody feels named. A per-body number — *this municipality, this health authority, this amount, this supplier* — is far smaller, and far harder to ignore for the person who signs it.
